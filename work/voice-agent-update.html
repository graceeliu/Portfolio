Designing a voice agent for senior populations and patients with dementia

Residents could find a voice agent warm without finding it young, and the one designed to sound cheerful was the one they cringed at. I translated desk research on how senior and dementia populations interact with voice agents and dementia communication best practices into three potential voices, then tested them in bedside interviews with patients to pick the one we use for our robot.

TLDR

The question
How should a robot speak — pace, pauses, wording — and how should it sound when it talks with senior populations or those with dementia?
What the research found
Dementia care says to leave long silences; voice-AI norms say a two-second gap feels broken. The two are compatible once you separate them: wait long before deciding the person has finished, and respond fast once they have.
What testing showed
In bedside interviews, residents picked the warm, mid-range female voice most often (3 of 8). The higher, brighter voice drew cringes and was called “too childish,” and the slower speaking rate worked for everyone.
Where it landed
Our robot now uses the warm, mid-range voice with slower, shorter, more patient pacing across the product.

The problem

Our robot talks to residents every day, and the team wasn't happy with how it sounded. The default voice had been chosen the way most voices are: it sounded pleasant to us. But the people the robot talks to are older, many with dementia, and most have some degree of hearing loss. A voice tuned for a normal-hearing adult in a quiet office can be harder to hear, harder to follow, and easier to dislike at a bedside.

So the question had two parts: what should Norbert sound like (pitch, gender, age, tone), and how should it pace a conversation (speed, sentence length, how long to wait for an answer). Neither had a ready answer, so I started with the literature.

What I found

1. Pitch matters more than gender, and mid-range wins.

Age-related hearing loss mostly affects high frequencies. Consumer text-to-speech is tuned to sound pleasant to normal-hearing ears, which tends to add bass and smooth over consonants; reviewers with hearing loss called smart-speaker voices "too bass-heavy." Very deep voices are harder to hear, and very high voices can feel shrill. In one robot study, an older user called a high-pitched voice "disturbing." The safest range is the middle: a mid-range female or a soft male voice.

2. Gender has no universal winner, but a female default is defensible.

Some studies found that older adults favored female agents, and female voices were preferred for assistive roles while male voices were preferred for authority roles; gender-ambiguous voices had mixed results (De Cet et al., 2025).

3. Middle-aged, calm, and warm beats young and chirpy.

A middle-aged voice reads as competent and safe. Very old-sounding voices lose intelligibility, and adults reject childlike voices since they can feel infantilizing, immature, and untrustworthy. Clinical agents used consistent, calm prosody and avoided overly cheerful affect.

4. The pacing advice seemed contradictory until I separated two moments

Dementia research says pauses of two seconds or more usually signal trouble in conversation, but that people with dementia need significant pauses to answer at all. Voice-AI research says human turn gaps are about 200 ms, so a two-second delay feels broken, and Alexa's fixed listening window cuts people off mid-answer. The two fit together once you see they describe different moments: how long to wait before deciding someone has finished, and how fast to reply once they have.

Designing three voices

Based on the literature reviews (middle-aged, calm, warm, slower rate), I designed three voice candidates in ElevenLabs that varied only along that open dimension.

Voice 1Warm, mid-range femaleThe option the research pointed to
Voice 2Higher, brighter, more animatedThe typical "friendly assistant" voice; tested whether the chirpy, young-sounding risk showed up here
Voice 3Lower, gender-ambiguousTested the gender-neutral option residents might want, and whether a lower pitch helped or hurt

Conversational pacing

  • Speak slower: 0.85–0.9× normal rate.
  • Keep it short: 5–9 words per sentence, one idea at a time, no multi-part questions.
  • Leave a beat: a deliberate pause after asking a question, so it lands before anything else is said.
  • Wait 3–5 seconds before asking again. Never treat a two-second silence as a no.
  • Learn each resident's timing: set the wait from how long that person usually takes to answer.
  • Re-prompt with patience, not repetition: "Take your time, I'm still here," and clear recovery lines like "Let's try that again" instead of "Sorry, I didn't understand."

Watching recordings of clinicians at the bedside backed this up. They opened with small talk, waited after each question instead of repeating it, and used short confirmations like "Okay, that makes sense" between topics.

Voice testing

I played the three voices for residents across four floors at the slower rate and asked which they preferred, while observing their physical reactions.

No preference 2
  • Voice 1 was the top pick. Residents said it had a "good balance" and sounded professional, "like a real doctor."
  • Voice 2 was the one they cringed at. Three residents winced, scoffed, or shook their heads, and called it "too childish" and "squeaky." The voice designed to sound friendliest was the least liked.
  • Voice 3 split the room. One resident liked the "more beef to the voice"; another found it "too manly."
  • The slower rate worked for everyone. Pacing wasn't a point of disagreement, so the team could ship it without a trade-off.
  • Warmth and youth are separate. Residents could find a voice warm without finding it young, which is what made Voice 1 work.

What changed

I recommended Voice 1 with the pacing rules above, and the team updated the robot's voice tone and speed across the product. The sample is small, so I framed the result the way the evidence supports it: a female default is defensible, not universal.
