Prompt personalization: what makes patients talk to a robot

Nursing-home residents were noticing our care robot, but saying nothing. I ran a crossover experiment to test whether a personalized first question would get them talking. Response rates were about even, but the conversations weren't: questions about the present opened people up, while questions asking them to remember the past were met with silence.

TLDR

The problem
Patients acknowledged the robot during ~70% of visits, but only by looking. Only about 10% of visits got a spoken response.
The test
Patients heard a generic check-in one day and a personalized opener the other, in random order, so every patient was their own control.
What we found
Response rates to the generic versus personalized openers were close (52% vs. 45%). The difference was in what came next: present-anchored questions ("How was your nap?") converted 65% of the time; questions requiring recall converted 32%.
What changed
A phrasing rule (ask about now, then follow their lead), an icebreaker library operators use today, and designs for present-anchored prompt buttons and a patient profile log.

The problem

Norbert is a care robot that visits patients in a skilled nursing facility, steered by remote operators. Nurses wanted to know one thing before trusting it at the bedside: will my patients be comfortable with it? Comfort shows up as conversation, and conversation is clinical data. A patient who talks tells us what they need: a request, a pain level, their mood.

After the team shipped waving and eye contact in July, patients noticed Norbert much more. But they still didn't talk to it.

+69%visits where patients acknowledged Norbert
+30%visits with any response over the same period
~10%of visits got a spoken response

The funnel was breaking between noticing and talking. The step right after acknowledgment is Norbert's first question, which was a generic "How are you today?" So this is what we tested.

The experiment

Hypothesis: a specific, personalized question is easier and more natural to answer than a generic check-in, so it gets more patients talking.

Arm A · Generic check-in

"Good morning, [NAME]! How are you today?"

Arm B · Personalized opener

"I like your jacket, where's it from?" · "How was your breakfast?" · "Did you play any sports when you were young?"

Arm B questions came from five categories, assigned at random and logged per visit, so I could see which kind of personalization worked, not just whether it did:

  • VisionWhat Norbert can see"What are you reading right now?"
  • Sensory / presentHow the moment feels"How was your breakfast?"
  • Past conversationA callback to a prior visit"Did your daughter visit today?"
  • ReminiscenceLong-term memories"Where did you grow up?"
  • ProfileFacts to remember later"Do you have any kids?"

Mostly answerable from the present Mostly requires recall

Why within-subject? In this population, differences between patients (cognition, hearing, mood, acuity) are larger than any plausible prompt effect. So each patient heard both openers on consecutive days, with order randomized (Group 1: A then B; Group 2: B then A), and served as their own control.

Personalized fails, generic alone reaches ≥18%Keep the generic check-in
No differencePhrasing isn't the bottleneck; look at voice, pacing, turn-taking

The result: a tie

Generic check-in 52%
Personalized opener 45%

Share of visits with a verbal response. Generic: 25 of 48 visits. Personalized: 22 of 49 visits.

Following the pre-set rules, personalization didn't earn a ship. Both arms also ran well above the ~10% baseline, most likely because a researcher attended every visit and directed operators, so I didn't count that as a win either.

The yes/no column only told me whether a patient spoke. The transcripts showed what they said, and that's where the two openers differed.

What patients said next

A generic "How are you?" usually got a one-word answer. A specific question often opened a real conversation, sometimes with patients who had never spoken to Norbert before.

"What are you reading right now?"

Patient held up her drawing to show Norbert and kept showing it for the rest of the visit.

Joint attention. A question about something in the room gives patient and robot a shared object to connect over.

"I like your denim jacket." (operator improvised)

A patient who previously openly mocked the robot asked, surprised, "You can see me?" She answered and waved at Norbert for the first time.

Reciprocity. A sincere compliment is a small gift, and people instinctively give something back.

"Did you play any sports when you were young?"

Later in the visit, the patient brought up basketball on his own. He's a Lakers fan.

Talking about yourself is rewarding. Self-disclosure activates the brain's reward system.

"Do you remember… Puerto Rico?"

Patient opened up about missing her family and wanting to go home, then offered to take Norbert there.

Old memories last longest. Recent memories fade first, which is why reminiscence is standard in dementia care.

Small talk also surfaced care needs that a yes/no check-in would have missed:

  • Meals: months of complaints, a hamburger served cold
  • Sleep: noise keeping a patient up at night
  • Feeling unwell: a cold
  • Personal care: help with her hair
  • A doctor: a request to see one

Present beats recall

When I recoded every personalized opener by what it asked of the patient, a clear split appeared. The question wasn't how personal the opener was. It was whether the patient could answer from what was in front of them, or had to dig a fact out of memory.

Present-anchored 65%
Requires recall 32%

Response rate. Present-anchored: 13 of 20 prompts. Recall: 7 of 22 prompts.

Worked

"What are you eating for breakfast today?"

"You have a lot of hats. Which one is your favorite?"

Didn't work

"Have you seen your brother recently?"

"I like your shirt, where'd you get it from?"

"Which hat is your favorite?" asks a patient to describe something present. "Where'd you get your shirt?" asks them to recall a fact about it. For older adults, many with memory impairment, recall is hard work and recent memory goes first.

Past topics weren't useless, though. Reminiscence worked well once a patient was already engaged, which is where the Lakers and Puerto Rico conversations came from. The order matters: start in the present, then follow them into the past.

Recommendations

We kept the generic check-in as the default for now. Personalization didn't fail; this round's real output was a phrasing principle, not a single winning script. I turned that principle into a visit flow:

  1. Sound familiarA calm, warm voice in the patient's language
  2. Keep it shortOne idea, 5–9 words
  3. Ask about nowThe meal, the nap, the hat
  4. Follow their leadThen the past: family, hometown, past experiences
  5. Listen longWait 3–5 seconds before trying again
  • Icebreaker library. All 42 tested openers, with exact wording, category, outcome, and transcript notes. Operators use it today to pick an opener that has already worked with real patients. This saves them time and effort from having to conjure up an opener on the spot.
  • Present-anchored prompt buttons. "Ask about the meal" (adapts to breakfast or lunch by time of day), "Ask about what's visible," and "Ask how they feel right now," so operators don't have to remember which category converts.
  • A patient profile log. Hobbies, hometowns, and family that come up in conversation get saved, so later callbacks are about things the patient actually shared.
  • A shared role split. The operator spots what's in the room and improvises; the voice agent delivers at an older-adult pace and carries the second turn; the library remembers what works for each patient.

The pacing and voice side of the flow became its own study: Designing a voice agent for senior populations and patients with dementia.

Limitations and open questions

  • Small sample size. About 100 visits and 42 personalized prompts. The present-vs-recall split is directional, not a confirmed effect.
  • The coding came after the data. Present vs. recall emerged from the transcripts. The next round should test it as a pre-registered hypothesis.
  • Researcher presence inflated both arms, and operators differed by floor. Personalized openers depend on operator skill more than the generic check-in does.
  • The sample leaned engaged. Many enrolled patients already talked to Norbert; the silent patients who motivated the study were the hardest to reach (asleep, door closed, declined).

The questions I left the team with:

  • How can we help operators spot what's in the room?
  • How can we help Norbert remember what patients share, for it to refer to this moment in the future?
  • How do we move from casual small talk to a pain or mood check without it feeling like a test? How do we make it genuine?
  • How do we approach conversations where patients share very private information?
  • How can we win over patients who haven’t said a word yet?
