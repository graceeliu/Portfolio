The neural mechanisms by which facial impressions influence memory

You judge a stranger's trustworthiness in well under a second, without deciding to. My master's thesis asked whether that snap judgment changes how well you later remember them — and found that trustworthiness and dominance don't act separately. Whether a trustworthy face is memorable depends on how dominant it looks.

TLDR

The question
Social trait impressions from faces, such as trustworthiness and dominance, form in a fraction of a second and influence job hiring, legal sentencing, and election voting. Most of what we know currently is behavioral. Far less is known about the neural mechanism, and almost nothing about how these impressions affect memory specifically.
The result that held
Trustworthiness and dominance interact rather than adding up (p = 0.002). At low dominance, a more trustworthy face is remembered better. At high dominance, the effect reverses — a more trustworthy face is remembered worse. At average dominance, trustworthiness does nothing. Faces are read as a whole, not as a sum of independently scored traits.
The result that didn't
Neither hippocampal nor amygdala activation during encoding predicted whether a face would later be remembered. The brain-behavior link I set out to establish was not present.
Future direction
The race-specific effects, which are the most interesting part, were unplanned exploratory analyses. They are worth testing properly in the future.

The question

From a glance at a face, people form instant judgments about character. Two dimensions account for most of the variance: how trustworthy someone looks, and how dominant. These impressions are formed rapidly and mostly unconsciously, but they seriously affect real decisions in hiring, courtrooms, and elections.

What's less studied is memory. If a first impression changes how strongly a face gets encoded, then trait-based bias isn't only about the judgment made in the moment — it's also about who you can recall afterward, which matters for eyewitness identification and for every situation where being remembered is an advantage.

My thesis asked two linked questions: Behaviorally: do trustworthiness and dominance impressions predict how accurately a face is later recalled? Neurally: do activation differences in the hippocampus and amygdala during encoding account for that, and do they predict individual memory performance?

Hypotheses:

  1. Untrustworthy faces will be remembered better than trustworthy ones. Threat-relevant information is prioritized.
  2. Dominant faces will be remembered better than submissive ones, because of perceptual salience.
  3. Hippocampal and amygdala activation during encoding will predict memory outcome.
  4. Untrustworthy faces will elicit stronger amygdala activation than average-trustworthy faces.
  5. The size of the trustworthiness-related activation difference will correlate with recall accuracy across participants.

Of these, one was partially supported as a trend, one was not supported, two were not supported, and the finding that survived best wasn't among them. The interaction was the strongest result in the thesis and it wasn't something I predicted.

Method

This was a secondary analysis of an fMRI dataset previously collected to investigate how race-based social categorization interacts with memory. I mapped normative trustworthiness and dominance ratings from a standardized face database onto the stimuli that the study had already used. Since the faces were chosen for a different study, they didn't cover the range of trustworthiness and dominance I would've liked for my question. If I were designing it, I'd have picked faces at the extremes of both traits. Instead, I worked with whatever variation happened to exist in the set, which reduced statistical power and limited how large an effect I could detect.

Study design

Thirty-one participants learned behavioral statements paired with eight male faces (four Black, four White) while in the scanner, then picked which face each statement belonged to. I used each face's normative trustworthiness and dominance ratings to test whether first impressions shaped what people remembered, using mixed-effects logistic regression on trial-level accuracy and ROI analyses of hippocampus and amygdala activity.

Why the error structure was the useful part

Errors split usefully into two: picking the wrong face of the same race, or of the other race. Same-race confusions were far more common, replicating a well-known pattern: people confuse individuals within a social category more easily than across one.

  • Within-category errors — picking a different face of the same race as the correct one.
  • Between-category errors — picking a face of the other race.

Analysis

  • Mixed-effects logistic regression on trial-level accuracy, using the continuous trait ratings as predictors with random effects by participant.
  • Repeated-measures ANOVA on mean ROI beta values across the three memory outcomes, with follow-up pairwise tests.
  • Median split on the trait ratings for the univariate imaging contrasts, applied across all database ratings so the threshold was identical for every participant.
  • Pearson correlations between each participant's behavioral trustworthiness slope and their trustworthy-minus-untrustworthy activation difference. This is the direct test of whether neural sensitivity to the trait tracks behavioral sensitivity.

Behavioral results

Trustworthiness: the predicted direction, marginally

Higher trustworthiness ratings were associated with lower recall accuracy; untrustworthy faces were remembered better (β = −0.268, p = 0.054). This is a trend in the hypothesized direction and it is not a significant effect. The distinction matters, and the honest reading is that while it's consistent with threat prioritization, it's insufficient to establish it here. Subsetting by race weakened it further in both groups, which is what you'd expect from halving an already small sample.

Dominance: no main effect

A weak positive trend, β = 0.053, p = 0.422. Dominance shapes how people are perceived but didn't independently drive what got encoded.

The actual finding: the interaction

Trustworthiness and dominance interacted significantly: β = −0.650, p = 0.002.

The slopes cross near mean trustworthiness, which means dominance matters most at the extremes of the trustworthiness dimension and barely at all in the middle.

Neural results

The central neural hypothesis didn't hold. Activity during learning didn't predict what was remembered, in either the hippocampus or the amygdala, and people whose memory was more sensitive to trustworthiness didn't show stronger neural sensitivity to it. This was likely caused by the small sample, few trials per condition, and averaging activity over whole brain regions, which can wash out localized effects.

However, in the localizer task, the amygdala showed trait-related differences that ran in opposite directions for Black and White faces: greater activation for trustworthy-rated Black faces, and greater activation for untrustworthy-rated White faces. Neither pattern appeared when faces were pooled, and the hippocampus showed nothing at any level. Since this was an unplanned race-specific analysis, it should be treated as exploratory only.

Conclusion

The central neural hypothesis failed, and the nulls were emphatic rather than borderline.

Encoding activation did not predict memory outcome.

Brain-behavior correlations were null. Participants whose memory was more sensitive to trustworthiness did not show larger trustworthy-versus-untrustworthy activation differences in either the hippocampus or the amygdala.

Plausible reasons: 31 participants, small trial counts once split three ways by outcome, and averaging beta values across every voxel in a large anatomical region, which discards spatial pattern information and dilutes any localized effect. Multivoxel pattern analysis or representational similarity analysis would test whether the information is there in a form that mean activation can't see.

Limitations

  • The dataset wasn't built for this question. Stimuli were originally selected to study racial categorization, not facial traits. If the dataset used faces designed specifically for my study, the same analyses would have had far more power.
  • n = 31. Small for fMRI, and even smaller for any subset analysis.
  • Trait ratings are normative, not participant-specific. The faces were rated by a separate large sample, so the analysis assumes my participants' impressions match the average.
  • Univariate analysis only. Mean beta values answer "how much activity and where." It doesn't tell us how information is represented in the brain.
