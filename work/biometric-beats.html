Biometric Beats: when your heart rate is the interface

Our team of three built a music system that reads a room's heart rate off smartwatches and picks the next song. Participants liked steering it without effort. One of them also became slightly paranoid that resting would be read as boredom, and this turned out to be the most interesting result in the study.

Type

TLDR

The problem
Picking music for a group is socially stressful ("aux anxiety"), and everyone else has no say short of complaining.
What we tested
Three ways for a group to steer the same music queue: passive heart rate from smartwatches, explicit up/down voting, and a human DJ reading the room.
The finding
Passive input removes the burden of choosing but adds a new one: you can't opt out of your own body. Involuntary input isn't lower-effort, it's differently effortful.

The idea

Most adaptive music systems personalize to one listener. We asked what happens when a group's real-time physiology steers a shared queue, and what changes about the experience when the input is something you don't consciously control. All three conditions used identical song-selection logic, so only the input differed:

ConditionWhat steers the musicWhat it asks of listeners
Heart rateThe group's average heart rate, song to songNothing conscious
VotingMajority up/down vote on a watchA deliberate judgment per song
Human DJA researcher watching the room's movementVisible reactions

I co-led the user study: the protocol, interview guide, and surveys. My teammates built the system.

What we found

1. Effortless, until you notice it

I became a little bit paranoid that I was causing the system to think I wanted a shift in the music when I didn't.

Participant, heart rate condition

Participants liked influencing the music without thinking about it. But resting mid-song felt like sending the wrong signal, and there was no way to say "this reading doesn't mean what you think." It also exposed a flaw in our model: treating every heart-rate dip as boredom, when rest is a normal part of enjoying music.

2. Binary voting suppressed the feedback it was built to collect

When a song was "solid but not my favorite," participants skipped voting entirely. Missing votes clustered in the middle, so the system heard mostly strong opinions.

3. The human DJ was too slow, so people performed

The lag between reacting and the music changing led participants to exaggerate their movements to be seen. The DJ's "observational" data was partly a response to being observed.

What I took from it

  • Passive systems need an override. Without a way to say "not now," effortlessness turns into self-monitoring.
  • Response options are a measurement instrument. If they don't cover how people actually feel, you get gaps that look like no data.
  • Small samples generate hypotheses, not rankings. With three participants and overlapping confidence intervals, the study can't say which method is best. The interviews are where the real insight came from, and next time I'd pick one shared outcome measure before building the system.
