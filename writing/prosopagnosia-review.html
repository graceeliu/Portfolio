A literature review of prosopagnosia

Category
Literature review
Course
Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, Maastricht University
Year
2023
Full paper
PDF

Prosopagnosia is the inability to recognize familiar faces despite intact vision and intact memory for objects. The developmental form affects roughly 2% of people and involves no brain damage; the acquired form follows lesions, most often in the right fusiform gyrus.

The review covers the neuroanatomy, the dissociation between being unable to perceive a face at all and being able to perceive it but not attach any meaning to it, and the social cost of the condition, including its association with social anxiety, which is easy to overlook when a disorder gets described as merely perceptual.

But, at its root, it is a measurement problem. Prosopagnosia is diagnosed by poor performance on face recognition tests, and several of those tests can be passed by people who have it because the tasks are solvable by matching non-facial features, which is exactly the workaround the condition produces in daily life. The test rewards the compensation instead of detecting the deficit.

The conclusion I found most interesting is that a 20-item self-report questionnaire has been proposed as the primary diagnostic route, because it correctly identifies almost all cases while the best-validated behavioral test does not. Self-report is normally treated as the weakest evidence available, but when you're investigating somehting that shows up very differently in each person, simply asing people about their own experience can beat measuring it.

I also deliberately wrote this review in an accessible, casual manner, since the audience is likely the everyday person trying to determine whether or not it describes them, or someone just starting to explore prosopagnosia.
