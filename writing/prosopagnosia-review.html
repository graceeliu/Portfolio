A literature review of prosopagnosia

Category
Academic papers
Form
Literature review
Context
Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, Maastricht University
Year
2023

Prosopagnosia is the inability to recognize familiar faces despite intact vision and intact memory for objects. The developmental form affects roughly 2% of people and involves no brain damage; the acquired form follows lesions, most often in the right fusiform gyrus.

The review covers the neuroanatomy, the dissociation between being unable to perceive a facial configuration at all and being able to perceive it but not attach meaning to it, and the social cost of the condition — including its association with social anxiety, which is easy to overlook when a disorder gets described as merely perceptual.

But the thread it ended up organized around is a measurement problem. Prosopagnosia is diagnosed by poor performance on face recognition tests, and several of those tests can be passed by people who have it — because the tasks are solvable by matching non-facial features, which is exactly the workaround the condition produces in daily life. The test rewards the compensation instead of detecting the deficit. Even the instrument built to fix that misses a majority of cases by one estimate.

The conclusion I found most interesting is the uncomfortable one. A 20-item self-report questionnaire has been proposed as the primary diagnostic route, because it correctly identifies almost all cases while the best-validated behavioral test does not. Self-report is normally treated as the weakest evidence available. When a construct is heterogeneous enough, asking people about their own experience can beat measuring it.

I also wrote it in a deliberately accessible register — it opens on a joke about two well-known people who have the condition — because the readership for a review like this includes people trying to work out whether it describes them.
