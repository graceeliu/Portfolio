A commentary on the differential development of face- and body-selective regions in the fusiform gyrus

Course
Independent study project, Maastricht University
Year
2023
Full paper
PDF

The fusiform face area and fusiform body area sit in nearly the same area of the right fusiform gyrus, with peak activation coordinates that are almost indistinguishable. Anatomical proximity that tight would ordinarily predict a shared developmental course.

The brain’s face- and body-selective regions overlap by 54–75%, so you’d expect them to develop together. They don’t. In a study across participants aged 7 to 32, the face-selective region grew in both size and selectivity through adolescence, while the body-selective region showed no relationship between size and age at all — it starts larger and the face area catches up to it. A third, adjacent body-selective region ran the opposite way again, shrinking with age. Three overlapping regions, three different trajectories.

My commentary asks what that overlap means for prosopagnosia, also known as face blindness. If face and body processing share so much territory, damage to one likely reaches the other. Yet people with prosopagnosia often rely on cues such as body features to recognize others because they can't rely on faces. So, this standard workaround may depend on a system that's also impaired. The original paper is about typical development and never raises it, but the overlapping anatomy makes it close to a testable prediction, and there is published evidence that people with developmental prosopagnosia do show impaired body perception, and less distinct face- and body-selective responses.

The same logic may explain why developmental prosopagnosia is so hard to treat. If these regions specialize through experience during a developmental window, training that comes later isn’t simply too weak. It’s too late. The problem is timing, not dosage.
