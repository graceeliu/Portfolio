A commentary on the differential development of face- and body-selective regions in the fusiform gyrus

Category
Academic papers
Form
Commentary on a primary research article
Context
Independent study project, Maastricht University
Year
2023

The fusiform face area and fusiform body area sit in nearly the same piece of right fusiform gyrus, with peak activation coordinates that are almost indistinguishable. Anatomical proximity that tight would ordinarily predict a shared developmental course.

The study I was commenting on found the opposite. Across participants aged 7 to 32, the face-selective region grew in both size and selectivity through adolescence, while the body-selective region showed no relationship between size and age at all — it starts larger and the face area catches up to it. A third, adjacent body-selective region ran the opposite way again, shrinking with age. Three overlapping regions, three different trajectories.

A commentary's job is to say what follows that the paper didn't, so I argued the clinical consequence. If those two regions overlap by 54–75%, then a lesion large enough to cause prosopagnosia should also damage body perception. The paper is about typical development and never raises it, but the anatomy makes it close to a testable prediction — and there is published evidence that people with developmental prosopagnosia do show impaired body perception, and less distinct face- and body-selective responses.

Which sets up the part I would still defend. The clinical literature notes that people with prosopagnosia compensate using non-facial cues, body features among them, and rehabilitation actively trains this. If the body-selective region is likely damaged alongside the face-selective one, the recommended workaround leans on a system that is probably also compromised. Nothing I read had put those two facts side by side.

It also suggests a reframe of why developmental prosopagnosia resists treatment. If category selectivity arises through experience-dependent learning during a developing window, and the condition disrupts that window, then training delivered afterward is arriving late rather than being insufficient — a timing problem rather than a dosage problem.
