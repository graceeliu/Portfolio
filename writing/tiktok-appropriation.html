Cultural appropriation in social-media fashion trends

Category
Journalism
Form
Reported magazine feature
Context
Magazine writing course, UC Santa Barbara
Sources
Four original interviews, plus published reporting

Written for a magazine writing class. It starts where a lot of readers already are — scrolling a feed full of trends, one of which is a lip combination a celebrity had recently branded and that had been common in Black and Latinx communities for decades.

The question the piece asks is narrower than whether that constitutes appropriation. It asks why the pattern keeps resolving the same way: a look circulates in communities of colour, gets rebranded, becomes a trend, generates a backlash cycle, and produces no consequence and no change.

The answer the reporting arrived at is that it isn't only about individual creators. The piece escalates through three levels — individual creators, then two fashion houses that crossed the same line at scale and faced no material consequence, then the platform itself. That last section is where it stops being a fashion story: beauty filters built around Eurocentric features, a recommender system that sorts by apparent race, and a content moderation classifier that flagged the plain statement “I am a black man” as inappropriate while statements of extremist self-identification passed without a flag.

That asymmetry is the strongest fact in the article, because it's a paired comparison rather than an anecdote — the same classifier, two inputs, opposite outcomes. It reframes everything above it. If the infrastructure treats one group's self-description as a violation, then creators being ignored when they push back isn't a series of individual failures.

The reporting decision I would defend is that one of my four sources thinks the premise of the story is wrong. I quoted her at length without rebuttal, and later quoted her saying she had never stopped to think about where these trends came from. Those two quotes together do more than any argument in my own voice.

Reading it back, the second half is essentially a bias audit of three production systems. At the time I thought I was writing about fashion.
