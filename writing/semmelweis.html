Ignaz Semmelweis and the genesis of handwashing

Category
Science communication
Form
Science history essay
Context
Professional writing minor, UC Santa Barbara
Full paper
PDF

In 1847, Ignaz Semmelweis noticed that one obstetric clinic in his hospital had a maternal mortality rate several times higher than the other, despite identical facilities and procedures. The only difference was who staffed them: physicians and medical students in one, midwives in the other. After a colleague died of an infection contracted from a scalpel cut during an autopsy, he made the connection — the doctors were arriving from the dissection room. He instituted handwashing with chlorinated lime, and he cut the death rate in his Vienna maternity clinic from as high as 18% to about 3% by making doctors clean their hands between autopsies and deliveries.

But the medical establishment rejected him, and he died in an asylum. Today, he’s called the father of infection control, but people often remember his discovery slightly wrong. He wasn’t asking for soap and water, but for disinfection with chlorinated lime, an idea that came decades before germ theory could explain it. This paper looks at why he was dismissed despite having the correct intervention and solid evidence, from the dominant “bad air” theory of disease to his own furious open letters. It also explains why that distinction still matters in hospitals, where hand-hygiene compliance is often below 50%.
