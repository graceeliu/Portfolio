Ignaz Semmelweis and the genesis of handwashing

Category
Science communication
Form
Science history essay
Context
Professional writing minor, UC Santa Barbara

Semmelweis noticed that one obstetric clinic in his hospital had a maternal mortality rate several times higher than the other, despite identical facilities and procedures. The only systematic difference was who staffed them: physicians and medical students in one, midwives in the other. After a colleague died of an infection contracted from a scalpel cut during an autopsy, he made the connection — the doctors were arriving from the dissection room. He instituted handwashing with chlorinated lime, and mortality in his clinic collapsed.

Germ theory did not exist yet; it arrived nearly four decades later. So he had a correct intervention, dramatic evidence, and no theoretical account of why it worked — and the medical establishment, operating under miasma theory, rejected him. He was young, his proposal implied that respectable men were unclean, and he answered dismissal by writing open letters accusing colleagues of murder. He died in an asylum, of an infected wound on his hand.

Two things made this worth writing rather than retelling. The first is that the story is usually told as a lesson about institutional stubbornness, and the more precise reading is about what happens when evidence arrives without a mechanism. His data were strong and his inference was right; what he lacked was a theory the field could hold the finding in. A correct finding with no available mechanism turns out to be very easy to dismiss.

The second is a correction the piece is really built around: Semmelweis is routinely misquoted. He wasn't advocating handwashing with soap and water — he was advocating chemical disinfection, with chlorine. That distinction changes how modern compliance figures should be read, since low handwashing compliance in hospitals partly reflects a shift toward alcohol-based disinfectants, which is closer to what he actually recommended than soap ever was.
